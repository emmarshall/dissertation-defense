Hello, thank you to everyone for being here. Today I am going to talk about my dissertation project and highlight some of what I found and then discuss my future directions with this work. My dissertation explores the nature of Fourth Amendment protections and legal decision-making in post-Dobbs pregnancy-loss investigations. It asks what happens when legal actors evaluate the evidence, the investigation, and the woman’s conduct with the benefit of hindsight.

While most people assume that pregnancy will invariably end in a live, healthy birth—

[CLICK]

unless the pregnant person chooses to intentionally end the pregnancy through abortion—

[CLICK]

the reality is that this ignores the large percentage of pregnancies that end in miscarriage or stillbirth. Quenby and colleagues set the risk of miscarriage at 15.3 percent of recognized pregnancies, and about one in every 170 births ends in a stillbirth after twenty weeks. And this creates a problem for the enforcement of laws that criminalize abortion or other outcomes of pregnancy, because it makes it particularly difficult to identify which pregnancies are potential criminal activity.

[CLICK]

While making this kind of clear distinction was always difficult for a variety of reasons related to the nature of pregnancy, it is particularly challenging today—as the technological innovation of medication abortion has further limited the outward or objective, easily quantifiable differences between voluntary and involuntary pregnancy loss.

[CLICK]

So the only real key distinction between an “abortion” and a “miscarriage” is the pregnant person’s state of mind at the time.

[CLICK]

And while historically abortion prosecutions were often hampered by the difficulty of collecting admissible evidence that could be used to clearly establish the pregnant person’s state of mind at the time, modern communication technology and the way that people live their lives digitally have effectively changed how and when these cases are likely to be investigated and pursued.

What facts and information will people use to distinguish between lawful and unlawful forms of pregnancy loss? What counts as an abortion depends on who is answering. Before a state can punish someone for an illegal abortion, it needs a statute that defines the prohibited conduct. Legislators often define abortion in religious or political terms that differ from how clinicians use the word. In clinical terms, a miscarriage is a spontaneous abortion. When a patient arrives for emergency care, no examination or blood test separates a self-managed abortion from a miscarriage. Then there’s how ordinary people make these judgments. As Finkel explained, commonsense justice represents what ordinary people think is just and fair. People think in narratives and prototypes. Studies demonstrate that commonsense judgments also tend to operate through how people construct narratives or stories that integrate both objective facts and subjective motives. Yet at the heart of the modern abortion debate is a lack of consensus about what an abortion even is, let alone how or when it ought to be criminally punished.

This widespread epistemological uncertainty creates an enforcement environment that is incompatible with the “fact on fact and clue on clue” methodology that suspicion-based review requires. Unlike the substantive decisional privacy rights once secured by Roe v. Wade, the Fourth Amendment’s protections are ultimately procedural. The Fourth Amendment works by defining the methods government officials can use to enforce the law—irrespective of a particular law’s substance. First, it prohibits only unreasonable searches and seizures.

Second, it establishes that probable cause is a precondition to a valid warrant.

In Illinois v. Gates, the Court described the magistrate’s task as deciding whether—given all the circumstances in the affidavit—there is a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place. No one, including the U.S. Supreme Court, can articulate precisely what the term probable cause means. For the most part, this ambiguity is by design. Probable cause has no singular definition because courts need it to both embody multiple meanings and perform a variety of different functions. This plurality of purpose distinguishes probable cause from other legal burdens of proof that courts are more than happy to define. Legal scholars have developed frameworks to bring analytical clarity to probable cause determinations. Taslitz offers a framework, defining probable cause as having four primary components: one quantitative, one qualitative, one moral, and one temporal.

[CLICK]

The moral component asks whether police have individualized suspicion, which Taslitz defines broadly as suspicion based on trustworthy evidence particularized to a person and situation, distinct from broad generalizations or stereotypes.

[CLICK]

Finally, the temporal component asks when police and courts must make their judgments. Do decision makers evaluate the evidence prospectively, at the time of the search, or retrospectively, after learning what the search revealed? A consistent theme in modern Fourth Amendment doctrine is the apparent disconnect between the forward-looking nature of the Amendment’s protections and the after-the-fact analysis that courts conduct in most cases.

[CLICK]

In United States v. Di Re, the Court explained that “a search is not to be made legal by what it turns up. In law it is good or bad when it starts and does not change character from its success.”

(Crespo, 2020, pp. 1276, 1286–1287)

In his analysis of probable cause doctrine, Crespo identifies four types of evidentiary claims that require different analytical approaches. Primary and ultimate facts are reports of direct observation, self-proving if the report is true, with the credibility of the person reporting them assessed separately. Thin scripts are probabilistic claims involving assessment of hit rates, like a drug-sniffing dog alert or statistical patterns of behavior. Narrative mosaics are qualitative claims presenting a holistic story of criminal implication that require qualitative judgment rather than statistical analysis. Finally, Crespo warns against what he calls mixed-up claims, where a qualitative claim is mistaken for a statistical one or the reverse.

[CLICK]

Despite the continued rhetorical insistence that Fourth Amendment burdens of proof are forward-looking in nature, the Supreme Court’s decisions have increasingly limited the number of settings that mandate any kind of meaningful before-the-fact review.

[CLICK]

The Fourth Amendment’s privacy-protecting function presumes that legal decision makers can reliably ignore irrelevant outcome information. Judges and other legal decision makers must disregard what they already know to have occurred and recapture the sense of uncertainty that would have existed beforehand.

The assumption that decision makers can evaluate evidence prospectively, judging what was reasonable at the time rather than being influenced by what happened after the fact, is central to Fourth Amendment doctrine but challenged by decades of psychological research. Hindsight bias is the well-documented tendency of people with outcome knowledge to make inaccurate judgments about the past.

For Pilot Study 2, I wrote three search warrant affidavits informed by the Pilot Study 1 narratives—a self-managed abortion, a non-criminal miscarriage, and a criminal miscarriage. In Pilot Study 2 and both main studies, people knew about the pregnancy loss from the start. What I varied was the information they learned after the police acted. The results were to show which of three scenarios produced the strongest outcome-knowledge effect on judgments of likelihood, foreseeability, and the subjective probability of guilt. I chose the non-criminal miscarriage scenario for Study 1. That scenario had the numerically largest increases in guilt and self-managed-abortion likelihood after the expected incriminating evidence—but the smallest change in foreseeability.

In Study 2, 320 people acted as jurors in a section 1983 case—a false-arrest claim brought by a woman who had been arrested for reckless feticide after a pregnancy loss at home. The prosecutor had declined the criminal case—concluding that the reckless-feticide statute was not intended to apply to a woman’s conduct concerning her own pregnancy. People in the no-information condition received no outcome information. People in the outcome conditions learned that the post-arrest investigation had determined that the loss was a self-managed abortion—or a non-criminal miscarriage—and were instructed to disregard that information when deciding whether the detective had probable cause. I also varied the woman’s circumstances and reported conduct—to present either a stereotypical or a non-stereotypical pre-arrest record.

In Study 1, self-managed abortion evidence did increase probable cause findings. In Study 2, people given self-managed abortion information blamed the plaintiff more than people given non-criminal miscarriage information. The Study 1 findings raise the concern that the commonsense assessment the law requires may depend partly on knowledge of what a search found.

The use of lay participants limits what the present studies can establish about judicial decision-making.

[CLICK]

Comparing judges and lay observers in a larger study—with tasks that more closely resemble actual proceedings—would help clarify whether judges are less susceptible to these hindsight effects.

[CLICK]

The use of a single type of fact pattern in each main study also limits generalization across other circumstances, search findings, and stages of pregnancy. The studies did not isolate evidence strength. In Study 2, many people did not correctly identify the outcome information in the case file, and several conclusions about likelihood judgments, punishment, and evaluations of the detective changed in the sensitivity sample.

[CLICK]

[CLICK]

I measured evidence relevance rather than manipulating people’s interpretation of it, and people in the main studies rated relevance after making their probable cause ruling. I adopted relative relevance after examining the registered results. The indirect effects also cannot establish that the sense-making process produced the bias.

The future of efforts to restrict and police abortion seems to be focused on politically palatable approaches: limiting access to medication abortion, and finding ways to sell it as particularly dangerous and grotesque.

[CLICK]

[CLICK]

[CLICK]

Anti-abortion advocates have also tried to turn the Comstock Act, an 1873 postal obscenity law, into a nationwide abortion ban that would stop the mailing of abortion pills. That would make the future of abortion criminalization look more like the war on drugs, and the first criminalization campaign, than the types of prosecutions that were more common in the period before Roe. These efforts present a lot of uncertainty about the future. These findings provide empirical evidence about how people interpret a pregnancy loss—and how they judge whether the surrounding facts justify government intrusion. Overall, more research on the intersection of abortion criminalization and Fourth Amendment issues is needed. And so my plan at this point is to spend the spring publishing this work and other projects to continue to apply for academic jobs that begin next year.
